# ICP & Persona Framework

> **How to use this file**
> This framework has two parts: the reusable structure (headers, fields, logic) and a worked example populated from EVCHARGESAAS — a B2B SaaS company in the EV charging space. When applying this to a new company, duplicate the structure, swap in your company context, and use the EVCHARGESAAS example as a reference for the level of specificity to aim for.
>

## The core qualification filter

Before any segment analysis, there is one filter that cuts across all ICPs:

**Architecture fit: API integrator vs. full-stack seeker**

The single most important qualification question is not size, segment, or budget. It is:

> *"Do you already have enterprise systems — CRM, invoicing, ERP — that you'd connect to us via API? Or are you looking for a platform to handle those functions too?"*

**API integrator** = good fit. They have infrastructure. You plug in as the specialist layer.
**Full-stack seeker** = structural mismatch. No commercial creativity fixes a product gap of this kind. Deals that die on "we need feature X" are usually mis-qualified on this dimension from the start.

Apply this question in discovery session one, across every segment.

## Segment 1: Large Utilities

### Who they are

The dominant energy provider in their home market — typically one of the top 1–3 utilities by customer base in a given country, serving millions of consumers, with revenue in the billions. They may have a secondary presence in a neighbouring country as a smaller provider.

At this scale and maturity, they are generally already committed to EV charging infrastructure. They are typically not evaluating *whether* to do it — they are deciding *how* and *with whom*. EV charging tends to be a supplemental and complementary product alongside their core utility services, built into long-term capital investment plans.

The strongest fits also have large renewable energy portfolios. For them, EV charging is not just a product — it's part of a broader energy management thesis. Smart charging and vehicle-to-grid (V2G) capabilities matter to them because of how they interact with the broader grid and renewable portfolio, not just as EV features.

**They are early movers.** They have skin in the game. They think in decades.

### Organisational profile

**Where EV charging sits:** A dedicated EV/mobility business unit, usually grown out of a new ventures or innovation team. If EV charging still sits inside the grid and network division, they are earlier stage than they appear — treat as a longer cycle.

**Primary champion:** Head of the dedicated EV/mobility unit. They own the initiative, have budget intent, and are your main relationship. But they are not fully autonomous.

**Deal influencers:**
- Grid and network division: generally interested, and tend to want pilots and proofs of concept rather than production purchases. They can slow or block a deal without being the buyer.
- CEO/COO: can kill a deal if the strategic decision gets elevated — and at this scale, it often does.
- Procurement/IT: become active once integration requirements are handed to them. Enterprise security, compliance, and system integration are non-negotiable requirements, not nice-to-haves.

**A useful qualifying question:** *"Where does EV charging sit in your org, and who does that team report to?"* The answer tells you maturity, autonomy, and likely cycle length.

### Buying trigger

Policy and regulatory drivers create urgency — but unlike oil and gas majors, utilities act from genuine strategic conviction, not just compliance. The "must do" is real. The timeline is serious.

Financial risk management on roaming settlements tends to resonate well at this level — they think in financial risk terms naturally and are more likely than CPOs to immediately grasp the exposure of millions in monthly roaming revenue dependent on partners who could go under.

### Disqualifiers

- Small regional utilities (e.g. German stadtwerke serving a single city or region): EV charging tends to be a "should do" not a "must do." Procurement and integration requirements can be enterprise-heavy relative to their strategic urgency, making the commercial conversation harder and slower to close.
- Utilities where EV charging still sits inside the grid division with no dedicated mobility unit: they tend to favour pilots and exploratory projects over production purchases. Treat as a longer cycle or pre-sales relationship.

### Personal stakes of the champion

**If the deal succeeds:** Career-defining. They are the person who led EV charging at a national energy incumbent. High visibility, high reward.

**If the deal fails:** Painful repetition. Another RFP cycle, another integration project, having to explain why the first vendor choice didn't work. The personal cost is process, not just outcome.

**Implication for sales and marketing:** They need to feel *safe choosing you* as much as they need to feel excited about the product. Risk reduction language — implementation track record, reference customers, integration experience — matters as much as capability language. Do not lead with features. Lead with confidence.

## Segment 2: Pure-Play Charge Point Operators (CPOs)

### Who they are

Companies whose primary business is owning and operating EV charging infrastructure. They have 500+ charge points in their network and are growing fast — or trying to.

They are often funded by investors (VC or PE) who are beginning to ask when the business becomes financially sustainable. Infrastructure is capital-intensive. Permitting and siting are slow and expensive. Utilisation rates are increasingly under the microscope.

Unlike utilities, capital *is* their constraint. Every month of operational drag is a month of burn.

### The 500 charge point threshold

Below this number, CPOs typically believe they can manage with Excel or self-built systems. The threshold is not a hard rule — it is a proxy for when operational complexity outgrows manual management. Specifically:

- Transaction volumes become hard to manage manually
- Roaming partner contracting and dispute resolution becomes a significant burden on the finance team
- Regulatory requirements require systematic handling, not ad-hoc responses
- Growth pace is constrained by operational weight, not market opportunity

**The real qualifying question is not size — it is:** *"Are you hitting an operational ceiling that is limiting how fast you can grow?"*

### Buying trigger

Two converging pressures:

1. **Operational scalability wall:** The systems and processes that got them to 500 charge points cannot get them to 2,000. Roaming disputes alone — overdue settlements, partner defaults, financial exposure — are becoming a finance team problem that is getting worse, not better.

2. **Investor pressure on utilisation:** The conversation has shifted from "how many charge points can you deploy" to "when do you stop bleeding?" Utilisation rates are now the metric that matters, and every operational inefficiency is visible in that number.

**The roaming risk argument:** EVCHARGESAAS takes the financial risk of roaming settlement defaults off the CPO's balance sheet. They see 100% of transaction value — none of it is at risk from partner default. When a CPO has millions in monthly roaming revenue and partners who are financially unstable, this is a concrete, measurable CFO-level argument. It is not always recognised until someone shows them the math or they get close to being burned.

**Note on roaming perception:** CPOs often perceive roaming coverage as a compliance checkbox — they need it so drivers can access their chargers. The real value (financial risk elimination) is underappreciated by less mature operators. The sales conversation should surface the latent financial anxiety around settlement risk, not just lead with coverage breadth.

### Investor-backed vs. other

A VC or PE-backed CPO behaves differently from a municipally funded or owner-operated one. The urgency, the internal selling language, and the decision timeline are all shaped by investor pressure. Qualify this early.

### Disqualifiers

- CPOs not using the full platform — partial adoption is a churn and reference risk, not a growth opportunity
- CPOs looking for a full CRM or invoicing solution (see core qualification filter above)
- CPOs where EV charging is not the primary business and urgency is low

### Personal stakes of the champion

**If the deal succeeds and the company scales:** Strong career signal — they helped build something that worked.

**If the company doesn't make it:** Career risk by association. They were part of a company that ran out of runway.

**Implication for sales and marketing:** They are not just buying software. They are buying a platform that has to help them hit the numbers that keep investors patient. They need to believe this accelerates their path to financial sustainability — not just solves an operational problem. Connect every capability to utilisation, efficiency, and investor narrative.

## Segment 3: E-Mobility Service Providers (eMSPs)

### Who they are

Companies with an existing subscription relationship with consumers or businesses, who are adding EV charging as a product extension. They are not pure-play EV companies — EV charging is one component of a broader offer.

**Examples of eMSP types:**
- Fleet mobility providers (e.g. DKV Mobility): offer fuel cards, tolling, car washes, fleet management — and now EV charging — to businesses via subscription
- Multi-modal mobility providers: subscription cards covering shared cars, scooters, public transport, and now EV charging
- Any company with a direct consumer or business relationship at scale that wants to add EV charging as a feature or revenue line

### The 25,000 subscriber threshold

This threshold is a **product fit proxy**, not a size filter.

Above 25,000 subscribers, an eMSP almost certainly already has enterprise CRM and invoicing systems to manage their customer base. EVCHARGESAAS connects via API as the EV charging layer. Clean integration, clear scope.

Below 25,000, they may be looking for EVCHARGESAAS to provide CRM and invoicing functionality too. EVCHARGESAAS cannot do this. The deal becomes a roadmap negotiation from day one.

**The qualifying question:** *"What does your existing customer management and invoicing stack look like? Are you looking for an EV-specific layer that connects to it, or a more complete solution?"*

### Buying trigger

EV charging is a **product extension decision**, not an infrastructure or operations crisis. They are adding a revenue line (or a retention feature) they believe will grow in importance. The internal champion is typically a product or commercial person rather than an infrastructure or operations lead — though this varies by how the eMSP is structured.

The integration must be clean, the service must work reliably for their customers, and it must not become an ongoing support burden. They are judging execution confidence more than capability breadth.

### Personal stakes of the champion

EV charging is a smaller part of their company's revenue and service offering. The personal downside of a poor choice is limited — this is a product bet, not a company bet.

The upside is real but moderate — being the person who successfully added a growing revenue line to the portfolio is a good career outcome, not a transformative one.

**Implication for sales and marketing:** Lead with integration simplicity and execution confidence. They are not looking to be inspired — they are looking to be reassured. Make the path to "live and working" feel short and low-risk.

| Product/Commercial Lead | eMSP | Champion | Early | Integration simplicity, execution confidence | Low downside, moderate upside |

## Discovery questions (apply across segments)

These questions qualify fit and surface the right conversation — use in session one:

1. *"Where does EV charging sit in your organisation, and who owns it?"* — reveals maturity and buying authority
2. *"What does your current tech stack look like for customer and operations management?"* — surfaces the API vs. full-stack question
3. *"What's the biggest operational constraint on your growth right now?"* — opens the scalability and utilisation conversation
4. *"How are you currently managing roaming partner settlements?"* — surfaces latent financial anxiety in CPOs
5. *"What does success look like for this initiative in 18 months — and who is measuring it?"* — reveals personal stakes and internal pressure
