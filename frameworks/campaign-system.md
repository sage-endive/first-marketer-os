# Campaign & Content System

> **How to use this file**
> Two parts: reusable structure and worked examples drawn from broad first marketer experience. This framework is more transferable than the ICP or positioning docs — the principles apply across B2B companies at similar stages, but should always be checked against the reality of the market and company. Swap in your company context for the specifics.
>
> Do not start any campaign before completing the pre-campaign checklist in Part 1. Running campaigns into broken infrastructure is spending money to generate noise.

## The first marketer's sequencing rule

> Before campaigns. Before content. Before channels.

**Get three things right first:**

1. **GTM clarity** — Who are you selling to, what are you saying, and how do deals actually get done? If this is unclear, no campaign will perform. See `/frameworks/icp-personas.md` and `/frameworks/positioning.md`.

2. **Data and infrastructure audit** — Are your systems working so that when you run a campaign, it is measurable and actionable? See Part 1 below.

3. **Sales alignment** — What is sales struggling with right now? Where in the pipeline are deals stalling? The answer tells you where marketing can have the fastest impact.

Only after these three does it make sense to build campaigns.

## Part 1: Pre-campaign audit checklist

> Run this before any campaign spend. Fix what is broken before adding traffic to a broken funnel.

### CRM audit

- [ ] Contact and company records are properly categorised (industry, size, stage, segment)
- [ ] Deal stages are defined and consistently used by the sales team
- [ ] Lead source is tracked on every contact and deal
- [ ] Open deals have accurate next steps and close date estimates
- [ ] Historical data has been cleaned and re-categorised where needed

### Marketing automation audit

- [ ] CRM and marketing automation platform are synced and working
- [ ] Form submissions are flowing into CRM correctly
- [ ] Email unsubscribes are syncing between platforms
- [ ] Lead scoring (if in use) is calibrated and not producing junk signals
- [ ] Campaign tracking parameters (UTMs) are set up and consistent

### Sales process audit

- [ ] Sales team is following up on inbound leads within an agreed SLA
- [ ] Hot lead response time is measured and acceptable
- [ ] Sales and marketing agree on the definition of a qualified lead (MQL to SQL handoff)
- [ ] There is a process for leads that go cold — not just dropping them

### Measurement baseline

- [ ] You can answer: how many leads came in last month, from which sources, and what converted?
- [ ] You can answer: where in the pipeline are deals stalling most often?
- [ ] You can answer: what is the average time from first contact to closed deal?

> **If you cannot answer these questions, fix the measurement before running campaigns. Otherwise you are flying blind and will not be able to improve.**

### Common things found broken

From experience across multiple first marketer situations:

- **Categorisation:** Contact and company data is inconsistently tagged or not tagged at all. Segmentation is impossible. Fix this first — it is unglamorous and essential.
- **Automation sync:** Marketing automation is not connected to CRM, or the sync is broken. Leads fall into a void. No nurture is possible without this working.
- **Lead follow-up:** Sales response time to hot inbound leads is too slow. This is a sales execution problem, not a marketing problem — but marketing takes the blame when campaigns do not convert. Requires top-down support to fix. If that support is not there, flag it explicitly and document it.

## Part 2: Where to focus first

### Talk to sales before building anything

The fastest way to have marketing impact is to solve the problem sales is already feeling. Before planning any campaign, have structured conversations with the sales team:

**Questions to ask:**
- Where in the pipeline are deals stalling most often?
- What objections come up repeatedly that you do not have good answers for?
- Which prospects went quiet after a promising start — and what do you think happened?
- What content or material do you wish you had in customer conversations?
- What does a good week look like for you, and what gets in the way of it?

Look for repeatable themes. One sales rep's frustration is anecdote. Three sales reps saying the same thing is a campaign brief.

### Possible high-leverage points in the funnel

Most B2B marketing plans overinvest in top-of-funnel awareness and underinvest in the moments that actually determine revenue. Depending on GTM motion and stage, some of the highest-leverage interventions worth considering are:

1. **The demo** — the moment where product-fit is established or lost
2. **The pilot** — the moment where technical and commercial confidence is built or broken
3. **The first 30 days of onboarding** — the moment where the customer decides whether they made the right choice

These moments are often owned by nobody in particular. Where they are a priority, marketing should own the materials, narrative, and structure — even if sales or customer success runs them.

> **Worth considering:** A well-running demo loop and a strong onboarding experience can do more for pipeline and expansion revenue than an awareness campaign, depending on where the current funnel is weakest. Fix what's broken before filling the top of the funnel with more traffic.

## Part 3: The demo framework

### What a good demo is

A conversation, not a presentation. The person giving it should:

1. **Research the prospect beforehand** — understand basic fit to product, the prospect's context, their likely use case, who will be in the room and what they care about
2. **Guide a consultative sales conversation** — ask questions, listen, adjust in real time. The demo follows the conversation, not a script.
3. **Tell a coherent story** — the narrative arc should come from marketing, centrally. Individual sellers should not be inventing their own story. The structure is standardised; the conversation within it is not.

### What a good demo does not do

- Run through features sequentially unless the customer asks for them specifically
- Ignore what the prospect said they care about in favour of the planned flow
- Present to a prospect who is not ready for a custom demo

### The two-stage demo model

Not every prospect is ready for a consultative custom demo. A generic first-touch demo — self-serve video, marketing-run webinar, or recorded walkthrough — should qualify interest and basic fit before a human enterprise seller invests time in a custom conversation.

**Stage 1 — Generic marketing demo:**
- Scalable, self-serve or marketing-run
- Covers the product story at a high level
- Qualifies whether there is enough interest and fit to warrant a custom conversation
- Can be a recorded video, a webinar, or a live but non-custom group session

**Stage 2 — Custom consultative demo:**
- Requires pre-research on the prospect
- Run by someone experienced in consultative sales conversation
- Follows the prospect's priorities, not a fixed feature sequence
- Outcome: mutual agreement on whether there is a fit worth pursuing

### When the demo is broken

- Prospect is not ready — they needed a generic first-touch first
- No pre-research done — seller does not know what the prospect values or needs
- Feature walkthrough replaces conversation — seller talks, prospect listens, nothing is learned
- Materials are not known inside and out — hesitation and uncertainty undermine confidence
- No clear next step agreed at the end

### Demo materials (owned by marketing)

- **Demo narrative doc** — the story arc, key messages by persona, the moments that matter
- **Discovery question bank** — questions to ask before and during to understand prospect context
- **Objection handling guide** — responses to the most common pushbacks, calibrated to persona
- **Demo environment** — clean, controlled, representative of real customer use cases

## Part 4: The pilot framework

### What a pilot is (and is not)

A pilot is a structured, time-bound engagement with a pre-agreed written goal. It is not:
- An extended free trial
- A proof of concept with no exit criteria
- An opportunity for the prospect to explore the product without commitment

### The written goal requirement

Before any pilot begins, the goal must be written down and agreed by both the prospect and the sales team. The goal should specify:

- **What is being tested** (technical integration, operational workflow, specific capability)
- **What success looks like** (specific, measurable outcome)
- **Who is responsible for what** on both sides
- **Timeline and milestones**
- **What happens at the end** — how the outcome is evaluated and what the next step is

> **Principle:** A pilot without a pre-agreed written goal is a free trial with extra steps. If the goal is not written down before the pilot starts, write it down now or do not start.

### Shared responsibility

When goals are set together, responsibility is shared. Neither side can walk away blaming the other for an ambiguous outcome. A pilot that runs to completion and meets its stated goal is a successful pilot — even if it does not convert to a sale. A pilot that converts without clear goals is a lucky sale, not a repeatable process.

### When pilots fail

- No written goal was agreed upfront — outcome is subjective and disputed
- Goal was set by the vendor, not jointly — prospect feels no ownership
- Prospect-side champion loses internal support during the pilot
- Technical pre-conditions were not met before starting — integration issues consume the timeline
- Pilot runs but nobody reviews the outcome formally — it fades without resolution

### Pilot materials (owned by marketing, run by sales or CS)

- **Pilot brief template** — captures the written goal, success criteria, responsibilities, and timeline
- **Pilot status update template** — regular check-in format to keep both sides aligned
- **Pilot outcome review template** — structured debrief that evaluates against the written goal and defines next steps

## Part 5: The nurture reality check

### Why nurture looks great in plans and underperforms in reality

Nurture campaigns are seductive in a marketing plan because they sound systematic and scalable. In practice, they frequently fail because:

- **Funnel spread is too wide early on** — contacts at wildly different stages receive the same content. Relevance is low. Engagement is low.
- **Content library does not exist yet** — nurture requires a sustained flow of valuable content. Most early-stage companies do not have it.
- **Segmentation is not possible** — because data is not clean enough to know who is at what stage

### When nurture actually works

Nurture works when:
- You have a clean, segmented list with known deal stage and persona
- You have enough content to sustain a meaningful programme (minimum 6–8 pieces per track)
- The content is genuinely educational, not thinly veiled sales material
- You have the patience to measure it over a 6–12 month horizon

### The pragmatic alternative: intense focus on key moments

Rather than building complex nurture sequences early, focus marketing energy on the highest-leverage moments in the journey — demo, pilot, onboarding. A simple keep-warm programme for prospects who have gone cold — company news, product updates, educational content, low-frequency email cadence — is more achievable and often more effective than a complex multi-track nurture system built before the content and data exist to support it.

> **Principle:** Do what you can actually run well. A simple programme executed consistently beats a sophisticated programme that collapses under its own weight.

## Part 6: Campaign sequencing across a full year

### The prerequisite: GTM clarity before campaigns

No campaign sequencing is meaningful without clarity on who you are selling to, what you are saying, and how deals get done. If these are unclear, resolve them first. Campaigns built on unclear positioning waste money and confuse the market.

### Sequencing logic

**Immediate (weeks 1–4):**
- Sales alignment conversations — find the repeatable themes
- Pre-campaign audit — fix data, automation, and lead follow-up
- Demo loop — get the demo working well before driving traffic to it
- Keep-warm nurture — simple, low-frequency, educational. Use all available content.

**Near-term (months 2–4):**
- Events strategy — if events are part of the GTM, plan now. Lead time is typically 9–12 months for major industry events. Decide which events, what role (sponsor, speaker, attendee), and what the goal is before committing budget.
- Content foundation — identify the 5–6 core content pieces that express the positioning and address the top ICP pain points. These fuel everything else.
- Pilot programme — if pilots are part of the sales motion, get the materials and process right before scaling

**Medium-term (months 4–8):**
- Channel strategy — once content exists and the demo works, decide which channels to invest in based on where the ICP actually is, not where it is easiest to advertise
- ABM — if the ICP is narrow and high-value, account-based marketing is more efficient than broad demand generation. Requires a clean account list and sales alignment.
- Case studies and references — the first 2–3 customer stories are the most valuable marketing assets at this stage. Prioritise getting them.

**Ongoing:**
- Competitive intelligence refresh (quarterly minimum)
- Content calendar — publishing cadence the team can actually sustain
- Sales enablement updates — keep materials current with product and positioning changes

### The stage dependency

Campaign sequencing changes significantly based on company stage:

| Stage | Primary focus | What to deprioritise |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-product-market-fit | Sales alignment, ICP validation, demo | Broad awareness, paid channels |
| Early growth (Series A) | Demo loop, pilot programme, first content | Complex nurture, events as primary channel |
| Scale-up (Series B+) | ABM, events, content at scale, channel diversification | Generic awareness campaigns |

## Part 7: Content principles

### Content fuels everything

Content is not a campaign type — it is the fuel that makes every other campaign work. Without content, nurture has nothing to send, sales has nothing to share, events have nothing to anchor, and paid channels have nothing to drive traffic to. Build the content foundation before investing heavily in any distribution channel.

### The content priority stack

1. **Positioning-level content** — the core narrative, the category story, the why-us argument. Expressed in the website, pitch deck, and sales narrative. Without this, nothing else is coherent.

2. **ICP pain point content** — educational content that addresses the specific problems your primary ICP is trying to solve. Problem-centric, not product-centric.

3. **Proof content** — customer stories, case studies, reference data. The most valuable content at early stage and the hardest to produce. Prioritise getting 2–3 strong ones early.

4. **Sales enablement content** — one-pagers, battlecards, objection handling guides, demo narratives. See `/frameworks/sales-enablement.md`.

5. **Thought leadership** — opinion, perspective, market insight. Useful for credibility and SEO over time. Not the first priority.

### Content principles

- **Educational over promotional** — content that teaches something is shared and trusted. Content that sells is ignored.
- **Specific over generic** — "How large utilities are managing EV roaming settlement risk" outperforms "EV charging trends 2026" for an ICP-targeted audience.
- **Usable by sales** — if sales cannot use a piece of content in a customer conversation, question whether it should be produced.
- **Sustainable cadence over burst** — publishing 2 strong pieces per month consistently outperforms 10 pieces in a launch month followed by silence.
