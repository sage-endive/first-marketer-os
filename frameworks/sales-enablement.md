# Sales Enablement Framework

> **How to use this file**
> Two parts: reusable structure and worked examples from EVCHARGESAAS and broader first marketer experience. Sales enablement is the bridge between marketing strategy and what actually happens in a customer conversation. Everything in this framework connects back to the ICP and positioning docs — if those change, update these materials.
>
> The best sales materials are written for the seller first, not the prospect. A document that helps a non-technical seller answer a technical question confidently is more valuable than a beautiful brand piece the seller does not know how to use.

## The core principle

**Sales materials fail when they try to say everything to everyone.**

The most common failure mode: a "kitchen sink" document that every stakeholder has added to, nobody has edited, and the result speaks to nobody specifically. Features listed without consequence. Claims without proof. No tension, no resolution.

The materials that work are the ones that:
- Solve a specific problem the seller has in a specific conversation
- Help fill a knowledge gap — technical, product, or competitive — that the seller cannot fill themselves
- Answer something a specific prospect asked that no existing material addressed
- Move the prospect through one defined step, not the entire journey

**Design every piece of sales enablement for one job. Not three.**

## The core sales enablement stack

These are the materials rebuilt at every company, regardless of product or market. If nothing else exists, build these first.

| Material | Job it does | Who uses it | When |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pitch deck | Initial screen — earn the next conversation | Seller with prospect | First or second meeting |
| Company/product one-pager | Leave-behind, internal sharing by prospect | Prospect shares internally | After first meeting |

## Part 1: The pitch deck

### What it is for

The pitch deck's job is to move a prospect through an initial screen — to establish enough credibility and relevance that they want to take the next step. It is not trying to close anything. It is trying to earn the next conversation.

This is a different design brief than most pitch decks are written to. When the job is "earn the next conversation," much of what typically goes in a deck gets cut.

### The narrative structure that works

**Problem → Solution → Proof**

This structure works because it mirrors how buyers actually make decisions:
1. They need to feel understood before they will listen to a solution
2. They need to see a credible solution before they will believe a claim
3. They need evidence before they will take a risk

**Problem:** Start with the prospect's world, not your product. Name the specific challenge your ICP is facing. Be precise — generic problem statements ("the EV charging market is growing fast") do not create the feeling of being understood. Specific ones do ("utilities managing roaming settlement risk across dozens of partners with no financial protection against default").

**Solution:** How you solve it, in plain terms. Not a feature list. A capability statement that connects directly to the problem just named. One slide per major capability maximum — if it needs more, it is too detailed for a pitch deck.

**Proof:** The evidence that you can deliver. Customer names (if available), specific outcomes, third-party validation. See proof formats section below.

### What to cut

- Feature lists that are not connected to a named customer problem
- Company history slides that do not build credibility relevant to the prospect
- Market size slides (the prospect knows the market — they are in it)
- Technology architecture detail (this belongs in the IT architecture doc, not the pitch deck)
- Anything that requires the seller to explain what the slide means

### Deck discipline

A pitch deck that requires the seller to apologise for its length has failed before the meeting starts. If every slide cannot be justified by "this earns the next conversation," cut it.

## Part 2: The one-pager

### What it is for

The one-pager travels without you. The prospect takes it, shares it internally, forwards it to a colleague who was not in the meeting. It has to work without a seller explaining it.

One job: give the prospect something they can use to sell you internally.

### Structure

**One side, four sections:**

1. **The problem** (2–3 sentences) — the specific challenge this prospect type faces. Written in their language, not yours.
2. **The solution** (2–3 sentences) — what you do, in plain terms. Not features. Capabilities and outcomes.
3. **Why us** (3–4 bullet points) — the differentiators that matter to this prospect. Pull from the positioning framework. Not generic claims — specific, verifiable statements.
4. **Proof** (1–2 customer references or outcomes) — the minimum viable credibility signal. See proof formats below.

**Contact details and next step** at the bottom. Make it easy to reach a human.

### Persona variants

A single one-pager cannot speak to a utility head of mobility unit and a CPO CFO equally well. If the ICP has meaningfully different personas with different priorities, build variants. The structure is the same — the problem statement, differentiators, and proof points are tailored.

## Part 3: Security & IT architecture document

### Why this exists

In enterprise B2B, IT and procurement are active deal participants. They enter mid-deal, they have specific questions, and if those questions are not answered quickly and credibly, the deal stalls. This document's job is to answer their questions before they become blockers.

This is not a marketing document. It is a technical and operational document that marketing owns and keeps current.

### What it covers

- **Data architecture:** Where does data live, who owns it, how is it segmented between customers
- **Security certifications:** ISO 27001, SOC 2, GDPR compliance status, penetration testing cadence
- **Integration architecture:** API documentation, supported protocols, integration patterns
- **Uptime and SLA:** Availability commitments, incident response process, historical uptime data
- **Vendor and subprocessor list:** Third-party services used and their compliance status
- **Data residency:** Where data is stored geographically — critical for EU enterprise customers

### How to build it

Work with the engineering or product team. Marketing owns the document and keeps it current — engineering provides the content. Update it when certifications change, when architecture changes, or when a prospect asks a question that is not already answered in it.

> **Note from EVCHARGESAAS experience:** Selling to large utilities means compliance teams are always active. This document is not optional — it is a deal requirement. Build it early and keep it current. A stale security doc in a live enterprise deal is a trust problem.

## Part 4: Objection handling guide

### What it is for

Sellers get stuck on the same questions repeatedly. The objection handling guide gives them confident, calibrated answers — so they do not have to improvise, and so the answer is consistent across the team.

### How to build it

Do not write it at a desk. Build it by:
1. Asking every seller: "What question do you get that you find hardest to answer?" Collect 10–15.
2. Grouping by theme — price, product gaps, competitive, trust, timing
3. Writing the best answer for each, calibrated to the persona most likely to ask it
4. Reviewing with a senior seller for tone and accuracy

### Structure per objection

**The objection:** Exactly as it is phrased in a real conversation
**Why they are asking it:** What is the real concern underneath the surface question
**The answer:** Direct, confident, not defensive. Acknowledges the concern before addressing it.
**The redirect:** The question to ask after answering, to move the conversation forward
**What not to say:** The answer that sounds reasonable but makes things worse

### Common objection categories in enterprise B2B

- **Price:** "You are more expensive than [competitor]" — almost always a value-articulation failure, not a budget problem. See positioning framework: reframe to total cost of wrong choice.
- **Product gaps:** "You do not have [feature] yet" — distinguish between "on the roadmap" and "out of scope." Never overpromise on roadmap. For out-of-scope gaps, qualify whether this is a deal-breaker or a nice-to-have.
- **Stability:** "How do we know you will be around in 3 years?" — answer with financials, customer names, backing. Do not be defensive.
- **Integration:** "How does this work with our [existing system]?" — have the IT architecture doc ready. Know the answer or know who does.
- **Timing:** "We are not ready yet / we need to think about it" — surface what "ready" means. Is it budget cycle, internal approval, a specific event? A vague timing objection is often a disguised trust objection.

## Part 5: Customer proof formats

### The case study problem in enterprise B2B

The traditional published case study — customer name, logo, quote, metrics — frequently does not work when selling to large enterprises. The reasons vary:

- Large corporations have legal and communications teams that block external endorsements
- Customers in competitive markets do not want to tell competitors what they are doing and with whom
- The ROI of participating is unclear to the customer — it costs them time with no explicit benefit
- Enterprise procurement cycles mean the person who signed off on the deal has moved on

> **From EVCHARGESAAS experience:** Selling to large utilities in Europe made traditional case studies nearly impossible to obtain. The organisations were risk-averse, legally cautious, and saw no commercial reason to participate. This is increasingly common in enterprise B2B and should be anticipated, not treated as a failure.

### Alternative proof formats that work in enterprise

**1. Anonymised metrics**
Aggregate or anonymised outcome data — "utilities on the platform process X million roaming transactions per month with Y% settlement accuracy." No customer name required. Requires good data infrastructure to produce credibly.

**2. Reference calls (private)**
A direct conversation between a prospect and an existing customer, arranged by the sales team. No public document. No legal approval needed (usually). The reference customer controls what they share. High trust signal — a peer talking to a peer. Requires active management of a reference customer programme.

**3. Logo display (permission only)**
The customer name and logo on the website or in a deck, without a quote or case study. Lower bar for customer approval. Signals credibility without requiring a full story.

**4. Third-party analyst validation**
Coverage or endorsement from an industry analyst or research firm. Requires investment in analyst relations but produces proof that does not depend on individual customer participation.

**5. Peer network introductions**
Facilitated introductions between prospects and existing customers through industry events, networks, or direct introduction. The proof happens in conversation, not in a document. Especially effective in tight industry communities (as the European EV charging market was).

**6. Outcome statements without attribution**
"One of our utility customers reduced roaming settlement disputes by X% in the first year." Specific enough to be credible. Anonymous enough to not require approval.

### The reference customer programme

For companies where reference calls are the primary proof format, manage this actively:

- Identify 3–5 customers who are willing to take reference calls
- Understand what each reference is willing to discuss (and what they are not)
- Brief them before calls — what is the prospect's context, what are they most anxious about
- Follow up with the reference customer after the call — close the loop, express gratitude
- Do not overuse the same references — rotation matters


## Part 7: Enablement principles

### Build with sellers, not for them

Sales enablement built without seller input gets ignored. Before building any material, ask two or three sellers to describe the conversation it is supposed to support. Build what they actually need, not what marketing thinks they need.

### Reactive materials are often the best materials

The one-pager built in response to a real prospect question will be more specific and more useful than the one planned in a content calendar. When a seller says "I wish I had something that answered X," build it immediately, then generalise it for the rest of the team.

### Keep it current or remove it

Stale sales materials are worse than no materials. A one-pager with outdated pricing, old customer names, or superseded product claims actively undermines credibility. Assign ownership to every material and set a review date. If nobody owns it, archive it.

### The seller knowledge gap test

Before publishing any sales material, ask: "Does this help a seller answer a question they currently cannot answer confidently?" If yes, ship it. If no, reconsider whether it needs to exist.
