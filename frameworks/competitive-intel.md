## Competitor 1: NexCharge

**Website:** NexCharge.com
**Profile:** Pure-play EV charging management platform. Strong technical sales motion and customer relationship management. Perceived as faster-moving and more commercially flexible than EVCHARGESAAS.

### Where they genuinely beat EVCHARGESAAS

- **Feature velocity:** Develops and ships features significantly faster. When a prospect has a specific feature requirement, NexCharge is more likely to have it or credibly commit to building it quickly.
- **Custom development:** More willing to build to customer specification. Higher commercial flexibility in deal structure.
- **Technical solution selling:** Sales team speaks the technical language well and engages engineering and IT stakeholders more effectively.
- **Customer relationships:** Generally perceived as more attentive and responsive post-sale.
- **Delivery of promises:** Where EVCHARGESAAS has struggled with roadmap slowness, NexCharge has a stronger reputation for following through on commitments. A significant trust signal in a market where vendor promises are frequently broken.

### Where EVCHARGESAAS genuinely beats NexCharge

- **Roaming breadth and depth:** EVCHARGESAAS's roaming network and roaming-as-a-service capability — including financial risk elimination on settlement defaults — is more developed. Meaningful differentiator for utilities and large CPOs with significant roaming exposure.
- **Migration experience:** EVCHARGESAAS has more experience migrating customers from legacy or in-house systems cleanly. NexCharge does not have referenceable experience when deals involve complex transitions from incumbent systems.

### How to win against NexCharge

Lead with roaming risk elimination and migration credibility. Do not compete on feature velocity or commercial flexibility — that is a losing argument. Reframe the conversation:

*"The question is not who develops faster. It is whether they will be there in year 3 of your contract — and whether their roaming settlement model protects your revenue."*

Use REFCUST1, REFCUST2, REFCUST3 reference customers to establish the trust gap. If those names resonate, the credibility differential does significant work.

### Watch for

- NexCharge improving financial stability and roaming capability — these are known gaps they will likely address over time
- Deals where the prospect's primary need is a feature NexCharge already has — often a mis-qualified prospect, not a competitive loss

## Competitor 2: WattWorks

**Website:** WattWorks.co
**Profile:** Full-stack, all-inclusive EV charging platform. Strong with small operators. Competes on feature breadth and speed, including built-in CRM, invoicing, and AI-adjacent features.

### Where they genuinely beat EVCHARGESAAS

- **All-inclusive capability:** Built-in CRM and invoicing. For operators without existing enterprise systems who need a complete solution, WattWorks is a better fit.
- **Small operator market:** Very strong with operators running fewer than 20 charge points. This is not EVCHARGESAAS's market.
- **Feature development speed:** Develops quickly and packages features accessibly. Good at selling ahead of capability.
- **Trendy positioning:** AI-adjacent features and modern UX. Lands well with less technical buyers who respond to narrative over substance.

### Where EVCHARGESAAS genuinely beats WattWorks

- **Enterprise integration:** EVCHARGESAAS's API-first architecture integrates with incumbent enterprise systems. WattWorks's all-inclusive approach creates a conflict — large customers already have best-in-class vendors for CRM and billing and need integration, not replacement.
- **Large deal capability:** WattWorks rarely wins utility or large enterprise CPO deals. The product is not built for that level of complexity, compliance requirement, or transaction volume.
- **Roaming depth:** Same advantage as against NexCharge.

### Important note on WattWorks's AI features

Treat with scepticism until proven otherwise. Trendy positioning in an emerging market frequently precedes real capability by 12–24 months. Ask prospects who cite WattWorks's AI features: *"What specific outcome does that deliver, and how do you measure it?"* Most cannot answer concretely. Track whether it develops into substance — do not dismiss it permanently.

### How to position against WattWorks

WattWorks appearing in a competitive evaluation is often a signal the prospect is mis-qualified. The right response is a qualification conversation, not a competitive pitch:

*"WattWorks is an excellent solution for smaller operators who need an all-in-one platform. If you already have enterprise CRM and billing systems you need to connect to, that is a different requirement entirely. Let us make sure we are solving the right problem."*

If the prospect genuinely needs an all-in-one solution, they may be better served by WattWorks. Winning a mis-qualified deal is worse than losing it cleanly.

## Competitor 3: SparkCore

**Website:** sparkcore.com
**Profile:** Services-heavy, consultative EV charging solutions provider. Strong on customer relationships and flexible delivery. Constrained by own technology scalability.

### Where they genuinely beat EVCHARGESAAS

- **Consultative approach:** Strong at listening to customer requirements and delivering solutions that feel tailored. Customers feel heard and well-served.
- **Flexibility:** More willing to adapt the offering to specific customer needs. Less rigid on product scope.
- **Relationship trust:** Builds personal relationships with buyers effectively. In markets where trust is the primary criterion, this is a real advantage.

### Where EVCHARGESAAS genuinely beats SparkCore

- **Technology scalability:** SparkCore has had operational challenges at scale — their platform has struggled with significant transaction volumes and network growth. Real risk for any customer planning substantial growth.
- **Roaming capability:** Same advantage as other competitors, although they are catching up.
- **Platform maturity:** EVCHARGESAAS is a more complete and mature software platform. SparkCore is stronger on services wrapped around a less developed core product.

### How to win against SparkCore

Surface the scalability question without making direct claims — let the prospect do the discovery:

*"As your network grows to X charge points, what does your platform need to handle in transaction volume and roaming settlements? Have you stress-tested that with SparkCore at that scale?"*

The goal is to make them ask SparkCore the hard questions, not to make claims that require substantiation.

## The real competitor: Stay with in-house

This is the competitive alternative that appears least on pipeline reports and matters most in enterprise deals. Build vs. buy and stay-with-incumbent decisions kill more pipeline than any named competitor — and are almost never tracked properly.

### Who chooses in-house

Large enterprises with significant internal IT capability, long planning horizons, and budget confidence to invest in bespoke development. They believe a bespoke capability will outperform any external solution long-term because it is built around their specific processes and data.

**The tell:** They are evaluating external vendors while simultaneously running an internal build in parallel. The vendor evaluation is partly genuine (setting requirements, understanding the market) and partly a hedge. At some point, internal political momentum swings one way.

### The EVCHARGESAAS case study

One major European player ran this playbook deliberately — evaluating multiple vendors including EVCHARGESAAS, using the process to crystallise requirements, while an internal build ran in parallel throughout. Outcome: they retained EVCHARGESAAS specifically for smart charging capability (which they could not replicate internally) while building their own system for everything else.

**What this tells you:**
- Smart charging is genuinely defensible — even a well-resourced internal team with years of runway chose not to rebuild it
- In-house decisions are rarely about price or features — they are about control, politics, and long-term strategic posture
- The right response is not a better product pitch — it is making the true cost of ownership and opportunity cost visible

### How to handle in-house conversations

Ask questions that make the prospect calculate the real cost themselves:

- *"What is the total cost of maintaining and developing your in-house system over 3 years, including engineering headcount?"*
- *"What is the opportunity cost of your engineering team building charging infrastructure software versus your core product?"*
- *"When a new EU regulation on EV charging comes into effect, who in your organisation owns updating the in-house system to comply?"*
- *"What happens to your roadmap if the two engineers who built this leave?"*

The goal is to make hidden costs visible, not to pitch EVCHARGESAAS features.

## Competitive dynamics by segment

Competitor relevance is not uniform across segments. This section is a hypothesis based on how each competitor's strengths map to segment buying logic and real-world results.

### Utilities

**Primary threat:** NexCharge, and in-house build. This is where NexCharge is most credibly positioned — utilities have the technical sophistication to evaluate feature velocity claims seriously, and the stakes are high enough that in-house build is a live alternative (see case study above).

**Lower relevance:** WattWorks rarely appears here — its all-inclusive, small-operator positioning doesn't match utility procurement requirements or scale.

### Oil/Gas & Retail Fuel

**Hypothesis, unvalidated:** This segment's defensive, budget-conscious buying logic (see ICP and positioning frameworks) may make it more price- and flexibility-sensitive than utilities. Two possible dynamics to watch for:

- **SparkCore risk:** A division running EV charging as a newer, less mature initiative — especially one without a dedicated mobility unit — may respond well to SparkCore's consultative, hand-holding approach, particularly if the internal team is small and inexperienced with charging infrastructure specifically.
- **WattWorks risk:** If the initiative is being run as a limited-budget side project rather than a fully resourced division, WattWorks's all-inclusive, cheaper-feeling positioning could appeal — even though it's a poor structural fit at scale. This may be a mis-qualification signal worth watching rather than a genuine competitive loss.

**Note:** Unlike utilities, in-house build is likely a lower risk here — this is not a division with deep internal software engineering capability the way large utilities or CPOs might have. 

### CPOs (Pure-play charge point operators)

**Primary threat:** NexCharge, particularly with fast-growing, technically sophisticated CPOs who value feature velocity and are comfortable with a more technical sales process.

**Secondary watch:** WattWorks may appear with CPOs below or near the 500 charge point threshold — often a sign the prospect is not yet ready for EVCHARGESAAS and is a mis-qualification, not a genuine loss (see ICP threshold logic).

### eMSPs

**Primary threat:** NexCharge, where the eMSP has strong technical requirements and wants a highly customizable integration.

**Lower relevance:** SparkCore's consultative, relationship-heavy model is a weaker fit here — eMSPs are optimizing for fast, clean, low-touch integration (see positioning persona notes), not a high-touch service relationship. WattWorks is plausible only for smaller eMSPs below the 25,000 subscriber threshold, where it may be a legitimate fit rather than a competitive loss.

## Discovery questions for competitive situations

Use these when a named competitor comes up in conversation:

1. *"What specifically attracted you to [competitor]? I want to make sure we are solving the same problem."* — reveals what the prospect values, not what the competitor claims
2. *"Have you seen [competitor] deployed at the scale you are planning?"* — surfaces scalability questions without making direct claims
3. *"How are they handling roaming settlement risk at your transaction volumes?"* — opens the financial risk conversation factually
4. *"What does your integration with [existing enterprise system] look like in their model?"* — surfaces the API vs. full-stack question in a competitive context
5. *"What happens to your contract if they are acquired or run into financial difficulty?"* — raises the stability question without making a direct attack
